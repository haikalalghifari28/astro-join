You have uploaded every document to SIMBG, waited for weeks, and then what appears on the dashboard is a "revision" notification or an application that has been sent back. The project is stalled, the contractor is waiting, and the schedule starts to slip.

A **rejected PBG** is a very common situation, and the good news is that the causes can almost always be identified and fixed. In practice, PBG applications are more often returned with correction notes from the technical assessment team than rejected outright. However, if the problem lies in zoning conformity, fixing documents is not enough and the building design needs to be adjusted.

This article covers the 6 most common reasons a Building Construction Permit (PBG) is rejected or sent back for revision, along with how to resolve each one.

**In short:** A PBG usually runs into trouble because (1) the plan does not conform to zoning, (2) land documents are problematic, (3) technical drawings are incomplete or inconsistent, (4) structural calculations and soil data are inadequate, (5) safety standards are not met, or (6) environmental and supporting documents are incomplete. The solution: read the revision notes carefully, correct the findings, and upload again through SIMBG.

### How Is a PBG Application Reviewed?

Understanding the flow helps you guess where your application is stuck. In general, the process covers submission through SIMBG, an administrative completeness check, evaluation of the technical planning documents by a technical assessment team from the relevant agency, issuance of a statement of technical standard compliance once the documents qualify, and finally issuance of the PBG after the levy obligation is settled.

The technical evaluation stage is where most correction notes appear: drawings are compared against zoning provisions, then checked for compliance with technical standards such as structure, sanitation, and fire safety.

### 6 Reasons a PBG Is Rejected and How to Fix Them

##### 1. The Building Plan Does Not Conform to Zoning (KKPR/KRK)

This is the most fundamental cause. Every plot of land has provisions recorded in its KKPR (spatial use conformity) or KRK (city plan statement), including the permitted function, Building Coverage Ratio (KDB), Floor Area Ratio (KLB), Green Area Ratio (KDH), building height, and Building Setback Line (GSB). If the drawings exceed these limits, for example a coverage ratio that is too large or a building too close to the road, the application will not pass.

**How to fix it:** Re-check your KKPR or KRK document before designing, then match the figures in the drawings against its provisions. If they do not conform, it is the design that must change (ground floor area, number of floors, distance to the plot boundary), not just the documents.

##### 2. Land Documents Are Problematic or Incomplete

In SIMBG practice, proof of land ownership or tenure is mandatory data. Common problems include the applicant's name differing from the name on the certificate, unclear land documents, leased land or land owned by another party without a land use agreement, and a land area that does not match the data in the drawings. A KKPR document also cannot replace proof of land rights.

**How to fix it:** Align the applicant's data with the land documents, complete a land use agreement if you are not the owner, and make sure the land area in the drawings matches what is stated on the certificate.

##### 3. Technical Drawings Are Incomplete or Inconsistent

Architectural, structural, and MEP drawings must be in sync. Classic mistakes include floor plans and sections that do not match, column sizes in the architectural drawings that differ from the structural drawings, floor counts that vary between drawings, or drawing scales and completeness that do not meet requirements. Unclear or incomplete drawings almost certainly receive revision notes.

**How to fix it:** Cross-check between disciplines before uploading. Make sure all required sheets are available, legible, and consistent in their key figures.

##### 4. Structural Calculations and Soil Data Are Inadequate

For multi-story buildings or long-span structures, the assessment team will review the structural calculations and the basis of the foundation design. Revision notes often appear when the calculation report is incomplete, load assumptions are unclear, the report does not refer to applicable standards, or soil investigation data is missing so the foundation lacks a solid basis.

**How to fix it:** Prepare a complete structural calculation report that refers to the applicable SNI standards, and attach soil investigation data. ##### 5. Safety and Technical Feasibility Standards Are Not Met

Beyond structure, the technical evaluation also covers fire safety, sanitation and clean water, and accessibility aspects such as access and stairs. A design that omits evacuation routes, a fire protection system appropriate to the building's function, or required facilities will be returned for correction.

**How to fix it:** Have your design consultant check conformity with safety and accessibility standards for your building's function, then update the related MEP and architectural drawings.

##### 6. Environmental Documents and Supporting Requirements Are Incomplete

Besides drawings and land, other basic requirements must be in place, for example environmental approval or documents appropriate to the scale of the activity (SPPL, UKL-UPL, or AMDAL), along with other additional documents depending on location and building function. Missing or non-conforming documents can hold up the process.

**How to fix it:** Make sure which environmental document applies to your building is clear from the start, and complete any other supporting documents the system requests before submitting.

### Five Steps When Your PBG Application Is Returned

1. **Read the revision notes carefully.** Do not simply re-upload without understanding every point requested.
2. **Group the findings:** administrative (data and documents), technical (drawings and calculations), or zoning (conformity with KKPR/KRK). Each group is resolved differently.
3. **Correct according to the notes.** For technical findings, involve a consultant so the corrections do not create new inconsistencies in other drawings.
4. **Seek clarification from the agency if a note is unclear.** Asking what a note means is faster than guessing and correcting repeatedly.
5. **Re-upload and monitor the status.** Slow responses are one cause of delays. In many regions, the service-time standard is counted from when documents are complete, while the time the applicant spends on corrections is not counted.

### How to Prevent a PBG Rejection from the Start

- Start from the **KKPR/KRK**, then design, not the other way around
- Do a **cross-check of drawings** between architecture, structure, and MEP
- Prepare **soil data and structural calculations** before submission
- Make sure the **applicant data and land documents** are consistent
- Use **consultant assistance** experienced with the SIMBG process in your area

### Frequently Asked Questions

##### Can a rejected PBG be resubmitted?

Yes. In practice, applications are usually returned with correction notes, and the applicant can fix them and upload again through SIMBG. If the cause is a zoning mismatch, the design must be adjusted first.

##### How long does the PBG process take?

The time differs between regions and depends on the building's function and complexity. In many regions, the service standard is counted from when documents are complete, so document completeness and how quickly you respond to revisions greatly affect the total time.

##### Do I have to use a consultant to apply for a PBG?

There is no general requirement to use a consultant in every case, but for multi-story buildings, commercial buildings, or drawings that need structural calculations, expert assistance greatly reduces the risk of repeated revisions.

### Conclusion

A PBG that is rejected or sent back for revision is not the end of the process. Almost all the causes, from zoning, land documents, technical drawings, structural calculations, and safety standards to environmental documents, can be prevented with proper preparation and corrected with clear steps.
